Le Boat design brief

November 8, 2026 · Revision 0.2 · Concept stage

Design and build a small RC sailboat inspired by a smaller version of the DragonForce 65. The boat should be practical to manufacture and assemble at home, using a Bambu A1 mini for printed parts and potentially fiberglass reinforcement. The exact size, construction method and performance targets remain to be chosen. The intended setting is neighbourhood sailing on the Love Park pond in Toronto. This is an unmanned model yacht: wind in sails provides all propulsion. Battery power is allowed for the receiver, rudder servo and sail-trim actuator only; no propeller, auxiliary propulsion or powered recovery drive is included.

Design intent

Develop the boat as a complete assembly: hull, rudder, keel, ballast, mast and rigging, with provision for radio control and internal equipment. Use Rhino to explore the geometry alongside manufacturing limits, shell thickness, component weights, strength requirements and assembly details.

This should also become a practical Hawa use case: investigate flow around a hull with its keel and rudder, while relating geometry choices to a boat that can actually be made. The boat design is its own project; it does not expand Hawa's current 0.5 development scope.

People, place and use

Design for someone who can carry the boat and transmitter from home, rig it at the pond edge, sail for a short session, recover it from dry land, and take it home without needing a car or waterside workshop. Carry protection, a removable rig, accessible controls and repairable parts belong in the design brief. Carry size, ready-to-sail time, session endurance and total cost need explicit targets after an equipment shortlist; none is yet a demonstrated capability.

The intended water is Love Park at 96 Queens Quay West. Google Maps was checked for the location; Waterfront Toronto's fact sheet gives pond depth as 200–600 mm (about 8–24 inches). The initial 18-foot claim conflicts with that source and is not used. Maps are not a depth survey. See site research for sources, operating-rule uncertainty and the required visit. Permission/access, actual water levels, obstacles and wind conditions remain unverified.

Provisional design envelope

These are proposed study inputs, not settled dimensions or approved plans.

Beam, hull depth and freeboard Open Hydrostatics, downflooding, heel and access layout

A published shallow point of 200 mm minus a proposed 150 mm draft leaves only a nominal 50 mm margin. That arithmetic is not evidence of route clearance. Measure and revise; a shorter fin reduces the ballast lever arm and may make stability, weight or rig targets incompatible. Do not solve draft by simply shortening a DragonForce-style keel without reevaluating the complete boat.

Whole-boat systems and interfaces

System Design work and interfaces
Hull/deck Displacement, trim/freeboard, shell joints, hatch coaming/gasket, reserve flotation, drainage and handling
Keel/ballast Lateral resistance, righting moment, secure ballast containment, grounding load path, replaceable/removable attachment and watertight trunk
Rudder Total draft, section/area, stock support, sealed penetration, servo torque/travel and mechanical stops
Rig/sails Mast support, boom, sail material/patterns, standing/running rigging, tension adjustment, sail balance, sheet routing and unobstructed release
Controls Compatible receiver/battery/actuators, linkage and sheet travel, current draw, switch access, antenna routing, failsafe and low-battery behaviour
Recovery/transport Visible hull, retained flotation, a reachable recovery point, shore retrieval equipment and protective cradle/rig stowage
Fabrication/maintenance Printable segments, tolerances, alignment, bonding/sealing, reinforcement, replaceable wear parts and access for repairs

No propulsion motor also means no motor to rescue a becalmed boat. Prove a shore-based recovery method before free sailing. Keep any temporary tether under control during near-shore tests; it must not create hazards or stand in for a workable sailing/recovery plan.

Manufacturing constraints

  • Manufacture at home with a Bambu A1 mini as the available 3D printer.
  • The manufacturer's A1 mini guide lists a nominal 180 × 180 × 180 mm build volume. Fit parts, joints, supports and adhesion margins in the actual slicer profile; nominal machine limits are not the usable dimensions of every orientation.
  • Treat printable shell thickness as a design constraint. Select actual values after choosing material, nozzle, print settings and reinforcement approach.
  • If the hull needs multiple prints, design their joints, alignment, sealing and assembly access as part of the hull rather than as an afterthought.
  • Account for the finished construction, including reinforcement, resin, joins and fittings, when estimating dimensions and weight.

The symmetric airfoil idea applies initially to the keel fin. Ballast shape and rudder section should be selected explicitly rather than assuming all underwater parts use the same profile.

Fiberglass and forming concept

The initial idea is to cover the printed construction with fiberglass and resin, then use a vacuum bag to consolidate/form the laminate.

The material was described in the initial brief as “75oz fiberglass.” Preserve this as an unresolved specification: confirm the intended fabric weight and units before selecting materials or estimating laminate thickness and mass. Do not silently interpret it as another fabric weight.

Confirm which parts receive reinforcement, the resin system, layer count and layup, and how the printed structure or tooling will be supported during bagging. The vacuum-bagging construction method is a candidate to test, not a settled manufacturing process.

Geometry and engineering questions

  • What size gives a useful small RC sailboat while remaining practical to print, assemble and equip?
  • What is the complete mass budget, including printed parts, laminate, resin, keel, ballast, rig, battery and electronics?
  • Where are the centres of mass and buoyancy for the intended draft, and what flotation and stability checks are needed?
  • How should hull shell thickness, local reinforcement and joints support the keel, ballast, mast and rudder loads?
  • How should the hull, keel and rudder geometry work together, including their attachment details and watertight penetrations?
  • What can be checked geometrically or with hand calculations, and what needs structural analysis, flow simulation or a physical test?

Flow analysis direction

Begin with clearly bounded studies of underwater appendages and, where useful, an explicitly submerged hull/keel/rudder assembly. Compare geometry variants at consistent physical conditions and inspect pressure, wake and vector-field slices.

A submerged single-fluid case does not represent the complete sailing boat at the water surface. Surface waves, flotation, heel/trim response and sail-driven motion need their own appropriate models or experiments. Likewise, CFD does not establish shell strength or printability; keep the manufacturing, structural and hydrostatic checks explicit.

First design outputs

  1. A dimensioned concept and assembly layout in Rhino, with the reference boat and intended size reduction recorded.
  2. A preliminary component mass budget and flotation/stability assessment.
  3. A print layout showing build-volume fit, hull segmentation and assembly joints.
  4. A proposed shell/reinforcement construction, supported by small print and laminate trials before committing to a complete hull.
  5. Parametric hull, keel, ballast and rudder geometry with clearly recorded inputs.
  6. A bounded simulation and physical-test plan tied to specific design questions.

Decisions to make next

Choose the overall size or target displacement; confirm printer build limits; select an initial construction approach; clarify the fiberglass specification; and estimate the equipment, rig and ballast masses. These decisions should guide the first hull geometry rather than treating the project as a purely scaled mesh.

Validation and evidence

Follow the build and test plan: site/access and recovery check; equipment mass budget; hydrostatics and stability; print/bond/laminate coupons; dry assembly and controls; controlled leak/flotation/heel tests; then short sailing trials within recovery reach. Record configuration and acceptance criteria before each test, including failures. Do not call the design validated because a CAD assembly looks complete or a CFD image looks plausible.

The first physical success is a recoverable boat that floats to its predicted trim, stays dry in a controlled leak test, responds reliably to steering and sail trim, and sails and tacks under recorded conditions without grounding. Speed, racing class compliance and optimization are secondary to an operable first boat. No tests have yet passed and no wind rating is assigned.

Documentation, licensing and co-creation

Maintain editable CAD and parametric source, neutral exports, drawings, BOM, print/laminate settings, assembly instructions and a dated build journal tied to revision IDs. The GitHub Pages site should explain the intent and document the actual making, including failed prints and rejected designs. It must distinguish planned work from completed work and renders from photographs.

Be explicit that the project is co-created with AI. Record tools, consequential suggestions, human checks and measured outcomes. AI is part of the experiment, not a hidden author or a substitute for validation.
